Timekeeping concepts implemented in BWQ

“Perfection is achieved, not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing to take away.”
— Antoine de Saint-Exupéry

This page explains the chronology model and time units used by the BWQ library. — back to demo

Astronomical and civil timekeeping rests on a few standard scales. TAI (International Atomic Time) is the continuous, uniform count of SI seconds since 1958, derived from atomic clocks. TT (Terrestrial Time) is TAI + 32.184 s, the theoretical ideal clock matching proper time at mean sea level. UT1 (Universal Time) follows Earth's irregular (!) rotation — a UT1 day varies by a few milliseconds from 86,400 s. UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) stays within ±0.9 s of UT1 by inserting leap seconds, breaking (!) the uniform SI-second count. J2000.0 (JD 2451545 TT) is the standard reference epoch for modern astronomical time scales, serving as BWQ's base for the calculation of times and durations.

All arithmetic in BWQ — durations, intervals, date projections — uses TT seconds from J2000. The Unix-millis epoch (1970-01-01T00:00:00 UTC) is used for system-clock I/O and local time calculations.

The problem with UTC time periods

Because leap seconds are inserted (or, in principle, could be removed) at irregular intervals, a UTC day is not always 86,400 SI seconds long. The last minute of a leap-second day has 61 seconds. This seemingly small discrepancy — just 1 part in 86,400 — can be destructive for any system that relies on precise duration arithmetic:

  • GPS and satellite navigation: a timing error of 1 μs causes a ~300 m position error. An uncorrected 1 s error would make GPS navigation impossible — the satellites broadcast their clock corrections in GPS time (continuous, no leap seconds), not UTC.
  • High-frequency trading: millisecond and microsecond timestamps determine trade ordering. A leap-second ambiguity can cause trades to be sequenced incorrectly across the discontinuity.
  • Network time (NTP): NTP handles leap seconds by slewing or stepping the clock. A step can trigger false-positive alarms in monitoring, disrupt authentication etc.
  • No extension: UTC cannot be uniquely extrapolated into the future (or distant past) because the leap-second schedule is unpredictable.
  • Non-monotonic real-time clocks: some systems repeat 23:59:59 or insert 23:59:60, causing confusion in logging, certificate validation, and file timestamps.

Actual outages caused by the 2012-06-30 and 2015-06-30 leap seconds include Linux kernel hangs (due to a bug in the hrtimer code), the Qantas check-in system going offline for hours, Reddit being unreachable, and various Java JVM soft-hang issues in applications that called System.currentTimeMillis() in a tight loop (cmp. the 'Leap second' article of Wikipedia).

BWQ addresses these problems by basing all internal computation on TT seconds with a J2000 offset: count of seconds since J2000 (double, D4-encoded) plus higher-precision ticks (nanobis) (long, 230 Hz) as the two native representations. UTC is handled via conversions with estimated delta-T. The library calculates and projects (pre-1958 and post-2026) delta-T values, allowing approximate conversions for ancient and future calendar dates where the precise leap-second schedule is unpredictable, unknown or not applicable.

Reference epoch: J2000.0 (TT2000E12)

All time scales in BWQ anchor to J2000.0 (also called TT2000E12), the Terrestrial Time instant of the 2000 January 1 noon (JD 2451545.0 TT). This is the fundamental epoch — every internal tick is measured as SI seconds (plus fractional values) since TT2000E12.

Era Day 0 and tick 0

The HT-16 timeline starts at tick 0, which precedes J2000 by 63,116,300,000 SI seconds (exactly 2000 years of 31,558,150 s each, as an intentionally rounded value for the sidereal year). Era Day 0 (ED 0 = JD 1721033) falls about 73,800 s after tick 0 (due to Earth's actual rotation), at a Sunday noon. The era-year notation Y0 refers to this same ticking (Y2000.0 = J2000), which is different from the proleptic Gregorian year 0 (1 BC) due to the sidereal count. The link between the HT-16 calendar with its time notations and Earth's rotation (UT1) is not broken, not even for future or distant dates, and the duration arithmetic is preserved.

CAT — suggested Civil Atomic Time

CAT (Civil Atomic Time) is a proposed uniform civil time scale that runs at exactly the TAI rate (continuous SI seconds, no leap seconds). Unlike UTC, CAT never inserts or removes seconds — it is monotonic and predictable indefinitely. CAT is based on J2000 and uses a timezone marker that follows TT (Terrestrial Time), not UTC. The displayed time therefore differs from UTC by up to roughly 30 s (the current delta-T between TT and UT1), without the complexity of leap-second announcements. Other time zones get marked accordingly (e.g. +11:59 ~ GMT, +12:59 ~ Amsterdam, +13:59 ~ Pretoria etc.). Because CAT uses TT-anchored timezones, the physical instant is always precisely defined — even if the HT-16 projection for delta-T is used, and even if Earth's rotation turns out to be significantly different from the projection, it remains more than enough in sync with the actual days and the underlying value remains precise.

For example, the J2000 epoch, which is defined as 2000-01-01T11:58:55.816+00:00 (UTC), can be written as 2000-01-01C11:59:00.000+11:59, which is exactly the same instant as 2000-01-01C12:00+12:00 or 2000-01-01C11:58+11:58 — the offset adjusts the civil clock face, not the underlying J2000 tick. The current convention 2000-01-01C11:59+11:59 is chosen because it gets closest to the current UTC system: 11:59 is the rounded delta-T value with the 12-hour offset between JD numbering and midnight.

As delta-T grows, the offset decreases: for example, the projected value for 2100 is 2099-12-31C23:59:31+11:57 (offset +11:57 instead of +11:59). If the actual delta-T in 2100 turns out smaller than projected, the community would simply pick the rounded minute that fits (i.e. 2100-01-01C00:00:31+11:58) — the offset is a conventional choice aligning daytime, not locked to the projection, and does not affect the underlying J2000 tick.

Weeks

The HT calendar defines weeks that are numerically identical to ISO 8601 week numbers for the 21st century. A week runs Monday–Sunday, and week 1 of any HT year contains the first Thursday of that year, exactly as ISO 8601 specifies. The crucial difference is that the underlying timescale is TAI-based (continuous) rather than UTC — there is no leap-second discontinuity to shift the week boundary. This means that for the entire 21st century, software using HT weeks will produce the same week numbers as ISO 8601, but the definition holds for any past or future date without depending on an unpredictable leap-second schedule.

13-month calendar

The HT weekly calendar can be projected onto a 13-month scheme where the first 12 months have exactly 28 days (4 weeks each). The 13th month ('Exember') sits at the end of the year and contains either 4 or 5 weeks (28 or 35 days), matching the HT week count for that year — nothing else is inserted. Because the first 12 months are exactly 4 weeks each, the day-of-week repeats identically from month to month, greatly simplifying recurring schedules. The 13-month layout is an alternative view of the same HT day count ('ED') — no additional leap rules are needed. The underlying era-day projection, in sync with Julian Days, is used as a convention for upcoming dates, but the physically precise timing does not depend on that: the time (hh:tt:ss) uses the same notation as CAT (Gregorian) and the weekly calendar (sidereal).

Time units

HT-16 year-seconds years

The HT-16 (Hemera Time) model uses a fixed sidereal year of exactly 31,558,150 SI seconds. One HT year is not a calendar year of varying length (like the tropical year) — it is a pure count of seconds, making it suitable for precise arithmetic over centuries. An era year Y satisfies: 

tt_seconds_since_j2000 = (Y − 2000) × 31,558,150

Unix millis (UTC)

Standard Unix time: milliseconds since 1970-01-01T00:00:00 UTC, which causes the mentioned UTC issues. It is used for conversions between J2000, UTC and current local time.

Nanobis (230 Hz)

The nanobis count is the finest-granularity time unit: 1,073,741,824 counts per SI second (230 Hz, about 0.931 ns per tick). Nanobis counters start at TT2000E12 and are used for monotonic linearised timekeeping via TimerNano.currentTimeNanobisLinearized(). They are not commonly used and therefore have to be derived from Unix millis.

Julian Days (JD)

The continuous day count used in astronomy. BWQ converts between J2000 ticks and JD via era days with delta-T projection or common delta-T estimation (especially for past dates).

Era Days (ED)

Era Days is the day count starting on JD 1,721,033 = ED 0 (internally using the projected UT day length: LOD drift/ delta-T: see below). The conversion applies an accumulated correction that accounts for the slowing of Earth's rotation: 

eraDayProj = 730,512 + ticks / 86,400 − correction

where 730,512 is the era-day count from ED 0 to J2000.

Length of Day (LOD) increase

Earth's rotation is slowing due to tidal friction etc. The HT-16 model projects a linear increase of 16 ms per millennium, referenced to the year 1900 (which is not accurate, but matches current LOD values quite well, and does not hurt at all, neither for the HT-16 calendar nor for the time notation): 

lod_micros(year) = 86,400,000,000 + (year − 1900) × 16

More details

Delta-T (TT − UT1)

Delta-T is the accumulated difference between uniform Terrestrial Time (TT) and irregular Universal Time (UT1, based on Earth's rotation). BWQ uses a piecewise polynomial model (DateFunc.deltaTEst_1600_2050) with 14 segments from 1600 to 2050, blending into long-term parabolic extrapolation beyond: 

deltaT ≤ −20 + 32 × ((y − 1820) / 100 )²   [commonly cited value as upper bound]

The internally used long-term formula differs: its reference year is 1900 (the parabola's top), considering the observed delta-T value of 0 near 1900 and aligning with both the HT-16 LOD reference and the generally assumed values for the year 0 (1 BC). The parabolic coefficient (30 instead of 32) and constant offset (+30 instead of −20) produce a good practical approximation for quite a range including years Y0 to Y3000: 

deltaT ≈ 30 × ((y − 1900) / 100 )² + 30   [used for practical reasons by BWQ, especially: y < 1600 and y > 2050]

Values between 1600 and 2050 use the piecewise polynomial; transition segments blend between the polynomial and this parabola for 2016–2100 and −1000–−600. The picked/DeltaT.java file (ported from van Gent's webspace) provides 15 historical formula variants for comparison: JPL/Horizons, IAU 1952, MS 1982, Stephenson-Morrison 2006, and others.

Delta-T-UTC (TT − UTC) and leap seconds

UTC differs from TT by the sum of the fixed offset TT−TAI (32.184 s) and the cumulative leap seconds (37 as of 2025). The conversion is simply: 

TT − UTC = 32.184 s + cumulative_leap_seconds

Cumulative leap seconds since 1972 are stored in a half-yearly lookup table (kLeapSecs_1970 in DateFunc.java), and interpolated for 1958–1972 via a “rubber seconds” linear ramp.

Hash62 Encoding (62-bit)

BWQ encodes a J2000 tick value, fractional nanobis, time-zone offset, and leap-second-of-day flag into 62 bits of a sortable long (cmp. convert4Hash62_d4). The bit layout preserves sort order: lexicographic ordering of the 62 bit encoding corresponds to chronological order and is fairly precise (not as precise as nanobis).
